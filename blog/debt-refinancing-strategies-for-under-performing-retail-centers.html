Debt Refinancing Strategies for Under-Performing Retail Centers

A practical guide to debt refinancing strategies for under-performing retail centers, focusing on cash-flow optimization, lender negotiations, and prudent capital markets solutions.

By Tate Dewey, Co-Founder of Valcrest Capital · November 14, 2026

By Tate Dewey, Co-Founder of Valcrest Capital, this article outlines practical debt refinancing strategies for under-performing retail centers. Valcrest Capital, led in part by Samuel Aureliano, Managing Partner, supports sponsors with institutional-grade CRE capital markets solutions while avoiding guarantees of funding or implied investment commitments. The aim is to help sponsors and capital providers navigate complex leverage decisions with discipline and clarity.

Direct answer: for under-performing retail centers, the most effective debt refinancing strategies begin with stabilizing cash flow and leases, then selecting the right debt structure—rate-and-term refinancings, cash-out refinancings, or subordinated financing—that aligns with the center's repositioning plan and timeline.

Understanding the current position of the retail center

Start with a transparent assessment of the center’s operating cadence. Key factors include occupancy and rent-roll health, lease expirations, concessions, and the quality of anchor tenants. Evaluate controllable operating expenses, CAM and net operating income trends, and the center’s turnover risk as leases roll. The objective is to translate operating risk into a credible forecast that lenders can validate through third-party reports and internal budgets.

Refinancing options for under-performing retail centers

Rate-and-term refinancing

Rate-and-term refinancings aim to replace existing debt with a new loan that offers a lower all-in cost or more favorable covenants, while preserving existing leverage. Benefits include improved debt-service terms, potential extension of maturity, and a cleaner covenant set if cash flow stabilization is underway. The key requirement is a credible plan showing how stabilized cash flow will support the new obligation, including an explicit repositioning path for the center.

Cash-out refinancing

A cash-out refinance uses existing equity to fund repositioning efforts—such as tenant improvements, repositioning of the retail mix, capital expenditures to modernize the center, or tenant-relocation costs. While this can accelerate stabilization, it increases debt service and requires a robust plan to drive occupancy and rent growth. Lenders will scrutinize the source of improved cash flow and the sustainability of the new capex-enabled performance.

Supplemental and subordinated financing

In cases where senior debt capacity is constrained, sponsors may consider supplementary financing such as mezzanine debt or preferred equity. These instruments can provide capital for repositioning while preserving senior debt capacity. The trade-off is higher cost of capital and tighter control covenants; alignment with the sponsor’s plan and a credible exit or recapitalization strategy are essential for lender acceptance.

Recapitalization and equity-driven restructurings

Recapitalization—bringing in new equity or a joint-venture partner focused on the asset’s repositioning—can complement debt strategies by strengthening the capital stack and underwriting stability. Lenders typically respond positively when new equity partners provide a credible source of future cash flow and a defined exit plan for the asset.

Pre-refinancing steps to optimize outcomes

  • Lease-up and retenanting plan: Develop a credible leasing strategy with timelines, concessions, and tenant-quality targets to demonstrate downstream NOI improvement.
  • Operating efficiency: Normalize controllable expenses, review CAM allocations, and tighten utility and maintenance budgets to lift net operating income.
  • Capex plan: Align capex with repositioning milestones and tenant improvement budgets, and separate non-recoverable vs. recoverable costs to aid lender review.
  • Pro forma rigor: Build forward-looking scenarios (base, upside, and downside) that reflect leasing momentum and macro considerations without over-optimism.
  • Data room readiness: Assemble rent rolls, operating statements, historic NOI, capex budgets, and third-party market analyses to facilitate lender underwriting.

Underwriting and lender criteria to expect

Lenders evaluate both current performance and the reliability of the repositioning plan. Common considerations include debt-service coverage relative to projected cash flow, collateral quality, occupancy and rent-roll concentration, and the sponsor’s track record with retail center repositioning. Key metrics lenders watch include the ability to service debt under moderate stress, the credibility of the lease-up schedule, and the risk profile of anchor tenants and discretionary spenders in the center.

Negotiation strategy with lenders

  • Prepare a lender-ready package: Rent roll, historical and pro forma NOI, detailed capex plan, occupancy schedule, and a clear repositioning narrative.
  • Provide scenarios: Present base, upside, and downside cash-flow scenarios to illustrate resilience under varied market conditions.
  • Propose structure and covenants: Discuss potential for interest-only periods in early years, reserve accounts, flexible amortization, and any needed waivers or modifications to existing covenants.
  • Bridge to stability: Consider a two-draw facility or a staged funding plan aligned with milestone leasing goals.
  • Third-party validation: Include appraisals, market studies, and an independent leasing letter or broker pro forma to support projections.

Common mistakes and how to avoid them

  • Overly optimistic rent-ups: Avoid assuming aggressive leasing velocity without credible market support or a detailed tenant-sourcing plan.
  • Underfunding capex: Neglecting critical improvements can derail repositioning and undermine lender confidence.
  • Incomplete data room: A disorganized set of documents slows due diligence and can derail term sheet progress.
  • Misalignment between plan and financing: Ensure the debt structure and covenants reflect the actual repositioning timeline and exit strategy.
  • Ignoring exit scenarios: Lenders want a credible path to stabilizing or recapping the asset; omit no clear exit or recap plan.

Practical next steps for sponsors

  1. Conduct a thorough pre-refinance assessment of cash flow, occupancy, and capex needs.
  2. Develop a credible repositioning plan with a detailed leasing timeline and budget.
  3. Prepare a lender-ready data room and a robust pro forma with stress testing.
  4. Identify potential debt structures (rate-and-term, cash-out, or subordinated) that align with the repositioning strategy.
  5. Engage a CRE capital advisor to facilitate lender discussions and market outreach, ensuring alignment with sponsor objectives and risk tolerance.

Conclusion

Debt refinancing for under-performing retail centers is most effective when it couples cash-flow stabilization with a credible repositioning plan and a well-structured capital stack. By prioritizing cash-flow optimization, presenting rigorous underwriting, and negotiating thoughtfully with lenders, sponsors can secure favorable terms that support acquisitions, developments, and recapitalizations in the CRE capital markets.
