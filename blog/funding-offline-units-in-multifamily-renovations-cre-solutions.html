Funding Offline Units in Multifamily Renovations: CRE Solutions

A practical, lender-friendly approach to funding offline apartments during renovation, with a focus on capital structure and prudent accounting.

By Tate Dewey, Co-Founder of Valcrest Capital · September 16, 2026

Authored by Tate Dewey, Co-Founder of Valcrest Capital. This article explains how sponsors can fund offline units during a multifamily renovation without compromising lender covenants or investor returns, through disciplined capital stacks and careful accounting. Valcrest Capital, where Samuel Aureliano serves as Managing Partner, supports disciplined capital solutions for CRE sponsors and lenders navigating renovation-period cash needs.

The Problem: Offline Units During Renovation

When a multifamily asset enters a renovation phase, a portion of the property inevitably goes offline. That downtime reduces near-term net operating income and can compress cash flow available for debt service. The central question for sponsors and lenders is not whether to renovate, but how to fund the offline units in a way that preserves covenant hygiene, protects liquidity, and maintains the long-term return profile for institutional equity buyers. The answer lies in a thoughtfully designed capital stack, disciplined draw practices, and robust accounting that separates capex from ongoing operations while aligning cash flow with project milestones.

Financing Framework: Aligning Capital With Renovation Milestones

Capital Stack Options

  • Senior debt for acquisition and pre-renovation operations: A traditional, secured loan that covers the asset’s baseline costs and preserves priority on cash flow for ongoing debt service.
  • Construction-to-permanent financing or bridge facilities: Short- to intermediate-term facilities designed to fund renovation work, with an eventual transition to long-term permanent debt once units are back online and stabilized.
  • Mezzanine debt or preferred equity: Supplemental layers to fill remaining capex gaps after senior debt, typically structured to stand behind the senior lien while adding necessary liquidity for capex and hold costs.
  • Equity or recapitalization (non-dilutive where possible): Strategic equity or recapitalization arrangements can provide additional cushion for renovation costs, while aiming to minimize sponsor dilution and maintain alignment with institutional expectations.
  • Cash reserves and sponsor-held liquidity: Pre-pledged reserves or sponsor liquidity lines to cover temporary gaps between cash inflows and renovation spend, helping to avoid covenant issues during draw periods.

In practice, the goal is to design a stacked structure that matches the renovation timeline with predictable draw-downs, while keeping senior debt service coverage and debt-Covenant metrics intact. This often requires early dialogue with lenders to set acceptable draw mechanics, reserve requirements, and milestone-based funding.

Interim Cash Flow Management

  • Operating reserves for vacancy and rent loss: Proactively budgeting reserves to cover rent shortfalls associated with offline units helps maintain serviceability of debt and reduces the risk of covenant breach.
  • Non-offline unit performance as a bridge: Optimized operations in unaffected units can generate steady cash flow to fund capex draw-downs, while renovation-driven vacancies are treated as a temporary asset impairment in cash-flow planning.
  • Transparent draw schedules tied to milestones: Align draws with tangible progress (e.g., permitting, framing, and unit turnover) to minimize misalignment between cash needs and lender expectations.
  • Contingency budgeting: Build a clear capex contingency to handle unforeseen renovation costs without triggering covenant concerns.

Intercreditor and Covenants: How to Avoid Default

  • Draft clear covenants around capex and occupancy: Specify permitted ranges for spend, approved change orders, and minimum occupancy thresholds during the renovation window.
  • Implement robust reporting: Regular, lender-friendly reporting on budget-to-actuals, milestone progress, and reserve balances helps early detection of potential covenant risk.
  • Use milestone-based funding controls: Tie each draw to a verifiable milestone or physical progress to reduce the likelihood of over-borrowing during the renovation.
  • Establish reserve funnels for rent loss and operating risk: Designate reserve accounts that can absorb temporary cash-flow shocks without triggering default scenarios.

Alternative Sources and Protecting Returns

  • Programmatic and non-dilutive capital: Consider government or programmatic financing options where appropriate to reduce overall cost of capital, while maintaining alignment with long-term value creation.
  • Partnerships and joint venture considerations: If project economics require larger capital pools, a carefully structured JV with aligned incentives can provide additional liquidity without compromising lender covenants.

Partnering with a Capital Advisor

Engaging a CRE capital advisor thoughtful about offline-unit funding can help design a capital stack that fits the asset’s lifecycle, forecasted rent-up, and rent-loss assumptions. Advisors can also facilitate lender negotiations, coordinate with lenders on covenant language, and help manage the draw process so funds flow predictably as renovation milestones are achieved.

Accounting and Treasury Practices: Making the Numbers Work

Capitalization of Renovation Costs

Renovation costs that extend the asset life, improve capacity, or add significant value should be capitalized to the real estate asset rather than expensed immediately. Establish a clear policy for distinguishing capital expenditures from routine maintenance. For large-scale renovations, keep a detailed job-cost ledger that ties each line item to a specific unit or building component. This clarity supports depreciation planning and helps avoid misstatements that could trigger lender or investor concerns.

Interest Capitalization and Draw Management

When financing is used during the renovation, interest on funds drawn to support capex and soft costs can be capitalized as part of the asset’s cost basis during the construction period. Track interest by financing source and by draw, ensuring capitalization is consistent with applicable accounting standards. Maintain separate ledgers for short-term financing and long-term debt so the ongoing interest expense reflects only post-stabilization periods unless capitalization is appropriate.

Budgeting, Change Orders, and Reporting

  • Baseline budget with a robust contigency: Build a precise capex budget with a realistic contingency to cover unforeseen scope changes without compromising cash flow or covenants.
  • Change-order governance: Require formal approvals for any scope change that affects cost or schedule, with impact assessed against the pledged reserve and debt covenants.
  • Monthly reporting cadence: Provide sponsors and lenders with monthly budget-to-actual reports, updated pro forma, and risk flags tied to occupancy and rent collection.

Tax and Depreciation Considerations

Renovation work creates new depreciation potential. Consider whether cost segregation studies or accelerated depreciation strategies apply. Always coordinate with tax advisors to ensure consistency with the asset’s overall tax strategy and to avoid misstatements that could mislead investors or lenders. This is not tax advice; consult a qualified professional for guidance.

Structuring Transactions: How to Assemble a Debt Stack That Preserves Returns

Option A: Traditional Senior + Construction-to-Permanent

Use a senior loan to cover acquisition and pre-renovation operating costs, paired with a construction-to-permanent facility to fund the renovation. As units are renovated and re-leased, the loan can convert to permanent debt. The key is to ensure draw timing aligns with milestone completion, and that pre-lease activity supports a stable post-renovation occupancy plan that sustains debt service without covenant stress.

Option B: Senior Debt + Mezzanine or Preferred Equity

When capex needs exceed senior debt capacity, add a mezzanine facility or preferred equity layer to bridge the gap. This structure preserves the senior lien’s priority while providing additional liquidity. Careful negotiation around pricing, triggers, and reserve requirements helps protect projected returns and keeps risk allocation aligned with sponsor and investor expectations.

Option C: Recapitalization or JV If Value-Add Becomes Core Strategy

In some cases, a partial recapitalization or a joint venture can supply substantial capital for the renovation in exchange for equity upside tied to rent growth and stabilized cash flows. This option requires clear alignment on governance, exit strategy, and distribution water-falls to avoid misaligned incentives during the project’s lifecycle.

Risk Management and Common Mistakes

  • Underfunding capex or contingency: Underestimating renovation costs or failing to reserve for change orders can create liquidity gaps and pressure covenants.
  • Ignoring rent loss during renovation: Failing to quantify and fund anticipated rent shortfalls can strain cash flow and threaten debt service coverage.
  • Weak draw controls: Unstructured draws driven by cash needs rather than milestone progress can lead to over-borrowing and covenant stress.
  • Poor governance around budget variance: Inadequate reporting and slow variance analysis impede early risk mitigation.
  • Misalignment of incentives among capital layers: If mezzanine or equity layers lack clear triggers and preferred outcomes, conflicts can undermine project performance.

Practical Checklist for Sponsors and Lenders

  • Define progress milestones and the corresponding funding triggers before close.
  • Pre-commit operating and capex reserves to cover rent loss, hold costs, and contingency needs.
  • Monthly budget-to-actuals, occupancy forecasts, and change orders prepared for both sponsor and lender review.
  • Set explicit occupancy, rent collection, and capex spend limits, with escalation paths for deviations.
  • Engage experts to help structure the stack and negotiate terms that protect long-term returns.

Conclusion

Funding offline units during a multifamily renovation requires a disciplined, lender-aware approach to capital structure and accounting. By thoughtfully stacking senior debt, construction or bridge facilities, and mezzanine or preferred equity, sponsors can cover capex and hold costs without compromising covenant integrity or investor returns. Aligning milestones with draw schedules, maintaining transparent reporting, and treating renovation costs with appropriate capitalization are essential practices for CRE sponsors navigating renovation cycles. With the right partners and a clear governance framework, renovations can unlock value while preserving financial discipline for institutional equity and lenders alike.
