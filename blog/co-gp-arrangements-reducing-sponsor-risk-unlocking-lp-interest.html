Co-GP Arrangements: Reducing Sponsor Risk, Unlocking LP Interest

A practical walk-through of co-GP structuring options that reduce sponsor risk while widening LP participation in commercial real estate.

By Tate Dewey, Co-Founder of Valcrest Capital · November 24, 2026

Authored by Tate Dewey, Co-Founder of Valcrest Capital.

In commercial real estate capital markets, a co-GP arrangement is a governance and economics framework that pairs the sponsor with a second general partner to share decision-making and risk. The objective is to reduce sponsor risk while widening LP interest—giving limited partners a broader stake in the upside without creating an overhang on the asset or platform. This piece outlines practical co-GP structures with an eye toward common sponsor considerations, lender due diligence, and the realities of CRE capital markets.

Direct explanation: what co-GP arrangements LP interest mean in CRE

A co-GP arrangement introduces a second general partner into deal-level or platform-level governance. The co-GP may participate in asset sourcing, underwriting, financing oversight, property management, or exit decisions, depending on the deal and jurisdiction. For sponsors, the goal is to share risk and governance with a credible partner while maintaining the ability to execute. For lenders and institutional LPs, a well-structured co-GP offers improved governance discipline, enhanced oversight, and clearer alignment on hurdles and outcomes. Importantly, these structures are not about guarantees or promises of capital; they are about risk-sharing, governance clarity, and economic alignment that can broaden LP participation without creating perpetual overhang.

Co-GP structuring options

The following approaches are commonly used to balance sponsor risk with broader LP access. They can be used individually or combined, depending on asset class, geography, and capital source.

Option 1: Parallel two-GP structure with defined responsibilities

  • Governance split: The sponsor leads deal sourcing and strategic direction, while the co-GP handles day-to-day asset management oversight, financing approvals, and compliance checks. Decision rights are codified to reduce overlap and ambiguity.
  • Economic alignment: Both GPs receive management fees and a carried interest structure that reflect their contributed scope. The co-GP’s carry is calibrated to the risk it assumes and the level of day-to-day control it exercises.
  • LP protections: Major decisions (refinancing, asset sale, extension of hold, new debt) require a defined pathway for LP input or consent, creating a clear check on sponsor-led actions.
  • Operational discipline: A joint asset-management framework and reporting cadence ensure consistency in performance metrics, risk controls, and communication with LPs.

Option 2: Defensible waterfall with deferred carry

  • Priority to LPs: Establish a preferred return for LPs before any promote to the GPs. This aligns risk and returns, providing downside protection for LPs during early hold periods.
  • Co-GP carry structure: The co-GP earns carry only after LPs have achieved the target return thresholds. If performance underperforms, sponsor exposure is cushioned by the waterfall and catch-up mechanics.
  • Risk mitigation: This approach reduces sponsor risk in underwritten scenarios and signals disciplined capital management to LPs and lenders.
  • Granular milestones: Tie milestones to asset-level or portfolio-level triggers (e.g., debt reduction targets, occupancy thresholds) to ensure transparent performance review.

Option 3: Governance protections and LP protective provisions

  • LP veto rights on fundamental matters: Include reserved matters such as major refinancings, asset sales above a certain threshold, new debt issuance, or changes to the core business plan.
  • Defined decision thresholds: Clear metrics and thresholds for debt levels, reserve allocations, and capital expenditures to prevent ad hoc sponsor actions.
  • Independent oversight: A governance committee with LP representatives or independent members to arbitrate disputes and ensure fiduciary duties are upheld.
  • Exit discipline: Explicit pathways for termination of the co-GP arrangement if performance, governance, or conflicts arise beyond agreed tolerances.

Option 4: Time-limited or on-ramp/off-ramp co-GP

  • On-ramp period: A defined initial period or asset count during which the co-GP actively participates and learns the platform, with performance reviews tied to continuation.
  • Off-ramp conditions: After reaching milestones or a fixed period, the arrangement can transition to a single-GP structure or evolve into a lighter governance model, reducing sponsor long-term exposure.
  • Clarity on economics: Ensure that any transition preserves LP protection and preserves agreed-upon return targets to avoid value leakage during the shift.

Option 5: Capital-backed (sidecar) co-GP alongside LP participation

  • Asset-level sidecar capital: The co-GP may contribute a modest equity slice or preferred equity in select assets or deals, providing economic alignment without general-miving risk escalation.
  • LP upside unlocking: LPs gain greater visibility and potential upside through an agreed-upon co-GP participation framework that doesn’t rely on sponsor leverage alone.
  • Structural safeguards: Maintain robust transfer restrictions, reporting, and compliance to ensure the sidecar remains aligned with the fund’s risk profile and regulatory requirements.

Key design elements to codify in any co-GP model

  • Governance and decision rights: Define who can approve budget revisions, debt issuances, asset-level changes, and major capex. Establish escalation paths and timelines to avoid paralysis.
  • Economics and alignment: Set management fees, hurdle rates, promote shares, and potential catch-ups that reflect the risk profile of the co-GP and its level of control.
  • Capital structure and liquidity: Clarify how capital calls are funded, credit facilities are managed, and reserves are maintained to support operations and mitigates liquidity risk for both GPs and LPs.
  • Protection for LPs: Include protective provisions on leverage, acquisition risk, and asset-level exit strategies, ensuring alignment with investor risk tolerances.
  • Transparency and reporting: Establish a cadence of quarterly performance, risk metrics, and compliance checks with standardized formats that LPs can rely on for decision-making.

Investor or lender decision points to evaluate co-GP structures

  1. Asset class and cycle dynamics: Office, industrial, multifamily, and mixed-use assets each carry distinct risk profiles. Ensure the co-GP structure fits the asset type and cycle stage.
  2. Capital efficiency and leverage: How will debt levels interact with the co-GP’s governance? Lenders will scrutinize leverage thresholds and reserve-calc rules tied to the co-GP’s decisions.
  3. Economic risk-sharing: Is the co-GP carry linked to a hurdle rate that protects LPs first? Does the structure recognize the co-GP’s control in a way that keeps risk proportional to responsibility?
  4. Control vs. discipline: Does the governance framework prevent sponsor overreach while preserving core execution capabilities? Balanced control reduces dispute risk and supports timely decisions.
  5. Tax and structuring: The chosen form (partnership, LLC, or SPV) has tax and reporting implications for both GPs and LPs. Ensure the structure aligns with the tax profiles of the stakeholders involved.

Common mistakes to avoid

  • Misaligned economics: Carry or fee arrangements that over-allocate to the sponsor or co-GP relative to control can create misaligned incentives and long-term discipline problems.
  • Ambiguity in governance: Vague delineation of decision rights often leads to paralysis or opportunistic behavior. Codify roles, thresholds, and escalation paths upfront.
  • Overly complex structures: While flexibility matters, excessive layering can impede execution and complicate exits or recapitalizations.
  • Insufficient LP protections: Inadequate veto rights or lack of independent oversight can erode LP confidence and limit capital access.
  • Unclear off-ramps: Without a defined path to dissolve or transition to a single-GP model, long-term sponsor risk may persist beyond what LPs anticipated.

Practical conclusion

Co-GP arrangements offer a practical toolkit for reducing sponsor risk while widening LP participation in CRE deals. The most effective implementations balance governance rigor with economic alignment, matching the deal’s risk profile to the appropriate level of joint oversight. For sponsors, a thoughtfully designed co-GP structure can improve risk discipline and access to capital, without implying guarantees or commitments that transcend the agreed framework. For lenders and institutional LPs, these structures codify decision rights, protect downside, and clarify how the upside is shared and realized. As markets evolve, the most durable co-GP models are those that emphasize clarity, accountability, and governance that supports timely execution across acquisitions, developments, financings, and recapitalizations.
