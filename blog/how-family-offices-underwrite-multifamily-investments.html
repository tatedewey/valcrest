How Family Offices Underwrite Multifamily Investments

By Tate Dewey, Co‑Founder, Valcrest Capital — a practical guide to what family offices look for when evaluating multifamily opportunities.

By Tate Dewey, Co-Founder of Valcrest Capital · August 23, 2026

If you want a direct answer to how family offices underwrite multifamily investments: they underwrite like conservative institutional equity providers — prioritizing sponsor alignment, downside protection, realistic cash‑flow assumptions, transparent reporting and financing certainty. Read on for the specific priorities, required materials and relationship factors that make a sponsor pitch compelling.

Underwriting priorities family offices emphasize

Family offices often sit between private individual capital and institutional investors in terms of process and expectations. Their underwriting priorities typically include:

  • Sponsor track record and alignment. Sponsors must demonstrate relevant multifamily operating experience, a clear equity contribution and alignment of incentives (meaningful sponsor equity, sensible fees and clear promote mechanics).
  • Conservative operating assumptions. Underwriting usually favors normalized occupancy, conservative rent growth, realistic concessions, and explicit unit‑turnover and turnover cost assumptions rather than aggressive market upside.
  • Downside protection. Focus on stress tests: vacancy spikes, higher expenses, slower lease‑up and interest‑rate stress. Many family offices want proformas that show resilience at multiple downside scenarios.
  • Capital stack clarity. Preference for simple, transparent capital structures. If mezzanine or preferred equity is part of the stack, terms and waterfall mechanics must be explicit.
  • Exit optionality. Clear hold‑period assumptions and credible exit scenarios (stabilized cap rates, sales comps, refinanceability) are essential for IRR and equity multiple sensitivity.
  • Governance and reporting. Regular, standardized reporting and LP protections (e.g., veto items, distribution priorities, audit rights) matter as much as projected returns.

Minimum materials family offices expect from sponsors

When approaching family offices for institutional equity, come prepared. Commonly requested materials include:

  1. Confidential Information Memorandum or executive summary outlining the property, opportunity, and ask.
  2. Rent roll and recent tenant ledger showing in‑place rents, concessions and delinquencies.
  3. Historical operating statements (P&L) for at least 12–36 months and year‑to‑date performance.
  4. Proforma stabilized operating statement with clearly stated assumptions for rents, vacancy, and expenses.
  5. Market comps and rent comparables, plus a brief market narrative (demand drivers, supply pipeline, employment trends where relevant).
  6. Sources & uses table and proposed capital stack (equity, mezzanine, preferred, senior debt assumptions).
  7. Business plan and timeline for renovations, leasing, and stabilization including estimated CAPEX and leasing velocity assumptions.
  8. Summary of financing terms available or in place, including lender commitments, rate assumptions, covenants and recourse mechanics.
  9. Title summary, environmental screening (Phase I if available), survey and any material third‑party reports (appraisal, engineering, market study).
  10. Sponsor diligence package: bios, track record, references, operating platform, organizational charts and sample reporting templates.

Presentation hygiene

  • Be concise. Family offices review many opportunities but appreciate clear, numbered pages and an executive summary.
  • Present sensitivity tables up front (NOI, cap rate, hold period, leverage) so decision points are visible.
  • Flag known risks and mitigants — families value candor over being surprised later.

Key relationship and behavioral factors

Beyond the documents, family offices invest through people. Sponsors should demonstrate the following relationship elements:

  • Transparency and responsiveness. Timely answers and accessible data build trust faster than presentations alone.
  • Governance clarity. Be specific about reporting cadence, audit rights, decision thresholds and investor communication plans.
  • Flexibility on structure. Families may prefer customized governance or hold longer than institutional peers — have alternate structures ready (co‑invests, preferred equity, bespoke reporting).
  • Alignment on timeline and liquidity. Explicitly discuss expected hold period and potential liquidity events: recapitalizations, dispositions, or refinances.
  • Credible references. Provide operator references and bank/lender references who can validate your workstreams.

Investor and lender decision points to anticipate

When family offices move from interest to commitment, these are common decision points they will scrutinize:

  • Sensitivity to interest rates and leverage. How does a 100–200 bps increase in financing costs affect cash flow and IRR?
  • Stabilization assumptions. Is the timeline for reaching stabilized occupancy realistic given local leasing velocity and tailwinds/headwinds?
  • Capital expenditure realism. Have you included soft costs, permits, and probable overruns?
  • Exit cap‑rate assumptions versus comparables. Do exit cap rates rest on comparable sales or on optimistic compression?
  • Governance vetoes and reporting triggers. What operational or financial triggers require investor consent?

Common sponsor mistakes to avoid

  • Overstated rent and absorption assumptions. Aggressive upside without supporting comps or leasing plans is a frequent deal killer.
  • Opaque capital stack. Lack of clarity on preferred returns, payment-in-kind provisions or lender covenants slows or halts diligence.
  • Insufficient sponsor skin in the game. Small sponsor equity contributions or misaligned fee structures raise red flags.
  • Poor responses to diligence questions. Delays, evasions or inconsistent answers erode confidence quickly.
  • Underestimating hold period and liquidity needs. Family offices will test scenarios where sale markets are muted and refinancing is constrained.

How to tailor a pitch to family offices

Practical steps sponsors can take when preparing a pitch:

  1. Lead with a one‑page investment thesis that directly answers risk, return and timeline questions.
    • Include key metrics: projected stabilized NOI, entry cap rate, exit assumptions, target hold, leverage and sponsor equity.
  2. Provide a simple sensitivity grid up front (NOI, cap rate, leverage) and a downside stress case showing breakeven scenarios.
  3. Offer governance options: preferred return, catch‑up structures, or co‑investment slots to accommodate different family preferences.
  4. Be explicit about reporting: monthly vs quarterly performance reports, KPIs tracked (occupancy, rent per unit, concessions, turnover, NOI variance), and timing.
  5. Prepare a concise diligence folder with prioritized documents so reviewers can escalate quickly to internal committees.

When to bring a capital advisor into the process

Speaking with a commercial real estate capital advisor can be valuable when you want to:

  • Identify which family offices are likely to move on a particular asset type and size.
  • Structure a capital stack that balances returns and governance to appeal to conservative institutional capital.
  • Polish your CIM and sensitivity analyses so they answer the most common underwriting questions up front.

Conclusion

Family offices underwrite multifamily investments with an institutional mindset: disciplined, conservative and focused on alignment and downside protection. Sponsors who present clear, conservative proformas, a transparent capital stack, credible governance and rapid responsiveness dramatically increase their chances of securing family‑office equity. For active transactions, thoughtful packaging and early structuring discussions with an experienced capital markets advisor can clarify which family offices are the right fit and how to tailor your offering to their underwriting criteria.
