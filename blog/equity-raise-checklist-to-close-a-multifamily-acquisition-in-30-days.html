Equity Raise Checklist to Close a Multifamily Acquisition in 30 Days

A practical, sponsor-friendly playbook to assemble equity commitments quickly and close a multifamily deal within 30 days.

By Tate Dewey, Co-Founder of Valcrest Capital · October 5, 2026

By Tate Dewey, Co-Founder, Valcrest Capital

This article provides a practical, sponsor-focused framework for assembling equity commitments quickly and closing a multifamily acquisition within a 30-day window. It reflects patterns seen in CRE capital markets where institutional equity, financing, acquisitions, developments, and recapitalizations intersect. Valcrest Capital helps sponsors navigate these dynamics, aligning expectations across institutional equity and financing channels without implying capital commitment or guarantees.

The 30-day equity-raise framework

This equity raise checklist close multifamily acquisition 30 days is designed to be a practical, sponsor-friendly playbook for moving from deal screening to funded closing. The intent is to compress the traditional due-diligence and capital-raising cadence while preserving rigorous underwriting and risk management.

Day 0–7: Align fundamentals and set the data room

  • Confirm deal thesis and targets: solidify asset class, market, submarket, unit mix, rent comps, and growth catalysts. Ensure alignment on the property’s lifecycle—stabilization, value-add potential, and exit plan.
  • Define the equity target and structure: determine target equity size, preferred equity vs. common equity, and any sponsor upfront economics. Establish scope for LP commitments and potential co-investors.
  • Assemble the data room and materials: rent rolls, operating statements, capital expenditures plan, tenant concentration, leasing pipeline, and historical occupancy. Compile market data, debt terms, and any existing financing.
  • Prepare a concise CIM and teaser: present the asset, sponsor track record, risk factors, and the proposed capital stack in a clear, investor-friendly format.
  • Outline the closing timeline: map major milestones, from LOI to funded closing, and identify potential lenders and equity partners to approach first.

Day 8–14: Build the capital stack and test the model

  • Develop the target capital stack: specify senior debt, mezzanine or preferred equity, and sponsor equity. Identify acceptable dilution ranges and waterfalls where applicable.
  • Construct multiple underwriting scenarios: baseline, upside, and downside. Stress key variables like occupancy, rent growth, capex, and debt costs to understand sensitivity.
  • Template outreach and initial conversations: initiate high-level discussions with credible institutional equity groups and other potential investors. Establish response timelines and disclosure expectations.
  • Prepare indicative terms and LOI templates: draft non-binding terms to accelerate discussions while preserving flexibility for both sides.
  • Coordinate with lenders early: if debt financing is part of the plan, begin pre-underwriting conversations and request term-sheet guidance to ensure lender compatibility with the equity plan.
  • Validate timing against closing mechanics: confirm whether equity needs to be wired before, at, or after debt funding and how this interacts with the closing date.

Day 15–21: Gather commitments and complete diligence

  • Obtain non-binding term sheets or LOIs from equity partners: ensure alignment on economics, governance, and exit scenarios. Capture any individual partner conditions or milestones.
  • Open the data room to prospective investors: respond to diligence requests promptly with organized documentation and clear ownership of data.
  • Finalize the financial model and pro forma: lock in key assumptions, including operating metrics, capex trajectories, debt service, and reserves. Prepare a pro forma narrative that communicates risk-adjusted returns.
  • Address sponsor-related considerations: confirm alignment on governance, decision rights, and reporting cadence to potential investors.
  • Coordinate parallel diligence tracks: while equity discussions mature, keep lender diligence on track to avoid bottlenecks at closing.

Day 22–30: Commitments, documentation, and closing

  • Secure binding commitments or firm term sheets: from at least a credible equity partner or a syndicate, with all material terms captured and agreed.
  • Prepare the closing package: subscription agreements, operating agreement amendments, loan documents, title, and insurance requirements. Ensure sponsor and investor signatories are identified.
  • Complete due diligence and comply with requirements: finalize environmental reviews, property inspections, and lender conditions; resolve any title or lien concerns.
  • Coordinate funding mechanics: align the timing of equity funding with debt funding and closing deposits to ensure a smooth transfer of ownership at close.
  • Execute closing logistics: coordinate with the closing agent, escrow, and counsel to finalize funds and transfer ownership with clear documentation.

Decision points for sponsors and capital providers

  • Is the equity risk aligned with the asset profile? assess sponsor track record, market reliability, and the asset’s planned value-add strategy.
  • Are the terms acceptable and executable within the timeline? review economics, governance rights, and any conditions precedent that could delay close.
  • Is there a credible path to stabilization and exit? examine occupancy targets, leasing velocity, rent growth assumptions, and estimated IRR.
  • Are there backup options if a partner withdraws? consider pre-existing relationships, reserve commitments, and flexible capital structures.
  • Is data room management disciplined and transparent? ensure auditors and investors can verify inputs and assumptions quickly.

Common mistakes and how to avoid them

  • Underpreparing data and due diligence: delays in responses derail timelines. Build a robust data room and a diligence-response playbook.
  • Overly optimistic assumptions: guard against aggressive rent growth or occupancy targets that strain the plan if market conditions shift.
  • Misaligned equity economics: ensure sponsor economics align with investor expectations and governance rights are clear.
  • Poor sequencing of commitments: avoid chasing too many small commitments at once; prioritize credible, well-qualified partners and establish binding milestones.
  • Inadequate contingency planning: have backup lenders and equity options documented to avoid last-minute gaps.

Practical close checklist and risk mitigation

  • Finalize a single, coherent capital stack: document all layers of debt, equity, and sponsor economics in one place.
  • Secure binding commitments before closing: obtain signed term sheets or LOIs with clearly defined conditions precedent.
  • Verify syndication and fund transfer logistics: align fund timing, wire instructions, and closing deliverables across all parties.
  • Coordinate signs and documents meticulously: ensure all agreements, consents, and amendments are ready for execution.
  • Implement a clean data closure: commit to a post-close data fidelity plan to support ongoing investor reporting and compliance obligations.

Conclusion

In commercial real estate markets, the interplay between CRE capital markets, institutional equity, and financing is essential to successful acquisitions, developments, and recapitalizations. A disciplined 30-day plan—grounded in a clear equity raise checklist and supported by a tightly managed data room, rigorous underwriting, and timely lender and investor engagement—helps sponsors move from screening to funded closing efficiently.
