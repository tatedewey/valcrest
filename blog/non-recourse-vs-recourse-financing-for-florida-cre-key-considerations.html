Non-Recourse vs Recourse Financing for Florida CRE: Key Considerations

A practical guide from Valcrest Capital on how non-recourse and recourse loan structures behave in the Florida market, with a focus on covenants, guarantees, and sponsor risk.

By Tate Dewey, Co-Founder of Valcrest Capital · September 26, 2026

Direct Opening: Non-Recourse vs Recourse in Florida CRE

In Florida commercial real estate, the distinction between non-recourse and recourse financing is not just a contractual label. It defines how liability is allocated between a property and its sponsor, and it shapes every step of a transaction—from acquisition and development to recapitalization and ongoing operations. For sponsors and lenders alike, understanding the practical implications of each structure is essential to align financing strategy with asset quality, sponsor credentials, and market dynamics.

Put simply, non-recourse means the loan is secured by the property itself, with limited personal liability for the borrower. Recourse means the borrower (often the sponsor or a guarantor) can be pursued for repayment beyond the property assets, subject to the loan documents. In Florida, where insurance costs, environmental considerations, and hurricane-related risk can influence cash flow, the distinction carries real meaning for pricing, covenants, and decision-making throughout the life of the loan.

Understanding the Structures

Non-Recourse Financing: Core Features

  • The loan is secured primarily by the property and its income streams. If the project underperforms, the lender’s recourse, if any, is typically limited to specified carve-outs rather than the sponsor’s general assets.
  • Carve-outs (bad boy carve-outs): Non-recourse structures include carve-outs such as fraud, misrepresentation, bankruptcy, intentional default, or other prohibited acts. These carve-outs preserve lender protection while preserving the sponsor’s shield from ordinary losses.
  • Covenants and compliance: Expect covenants that require the borrower to maintain insurance, pay real estate taxes, preserve critical licenses, keep the property in good condition, and not incur new liens without consent. Performance milestones and reserve requirements are common for development or value-add projects.
  • Pricing and leverage: Non-recourse borrowers typically face higher pricing or tighter terms for risk control, especially on development, redevelopment, or markets with elevated volatility. Leverage is structured to reflect project cash flow reliability and sponsor track record.
  • Guaranties: Personal guarantees are generally not required for the core loan in a clean non-recourse package. Where used, guarantees tend to be limited in scope and tied to specific performance or timing events rather than broad liability.

Recourse Financing: Core Features

  • Recourse loans allow lenders to pursue the borrower or guarantor(s) for repayment beyond the collateral, which can materially affect the sponsor’s balance sheet and liquidity.
  • Guarantees and covenants: Recourse structures commonly involve explicit personal or corporate guarantees, broader financial reporting, and tighter post-closing conditions. In practice, sponsors should expect more detailed cash-flow testing and ongoing compliance with financial covenants.
  • Pricing and leverage: In some cases, recourse financing can offer favorable pricing or terms, particularly for projects where the sponsor has a strong credit profile, a robust capital plan, or significant equity at risk. For development or complex value-add transactions, recourse can align lender incentives with sponsor execution risk.
  • Risk allocation: While recourse can reduce the lender’s perceived risk through guarantee alignment, it elevates sponsor risk and requires careful balance between control, liquidity, and capital structure.

Florida-Specific Considerations

Florida presents a unique macro-environment for CRE financing. Hurricanes, flood risk, and insurance volatility shape property operating costs and debt service reliability. Local tax assessments, insurance requirements, and flood-plain exposure influence underwriting beyond the pure cash-flow math. These factors interact with loan structure in several practical ways:

  • Insurance and resilience: Lenders will scrutinize reserve levels for deductible coverage, storm surge protection, and property-level improvements that reduce risk exposure. A robust reserve and risk-mitigation plan can tilt a non-recourse lender toward more favorable terms in stabilized assets.
  • Cross-collateral and cross-default considerations: Florida deals, especially portfolios or properties with shared tenants or shared financing, may trigger cross-collateral provisions. Sponsors should anticipate how cross-default and cross-collateral language affects risk across assets and capital efficiency.
  • Asset type sensitivity: Industrial and multifamily assets with visible demand drivers in Florida’s growing markets often support stronger non-recourse terms, provided the sponsor has demonstrated performance. Office and mixed-use properties may face more nuanced covenants depending on market liquidity and tenant risk.
  • Development and permit risk: In development or significant redevelopment, lenders frequently require recourse or enhanced guarantees to align incentives with project delivery and regulatory milestones.

For sponsors, the choice between non-recourse and recourse should reflect not only the asset type but also the sponsor’s execution capabilities, capital plan, and timing. In Florida, where insurance costs and regulatory landscapes can shift quickly, a structure that preserves optionality and resilience is often valuable.

Covenants, Guarantees, and Risk Trade-offs

Covenants and Performance Tests

Regardless of structure, lenders will impose covenants tied to cash flow, reserve levels, and asset performance. Typical elements include:

  • Debt service coverage ratio (DSCR) targets and debt yield thresholds
  • Required reserve funds for debt service, repairs, and capital expenditures
  • Maintenance of insurance, tax status, and licenses
  • Restrictions on asset-level liens, substitutions, and dispositions without consent
  • Reporting obligations, including financial statements, rent rolls, and operating metrics

In non-recourse structures, these covenants are designed to protect the lender while preserving the sponsor’s shield from personal liability. In recourse structures, covenants are often more granular, and triggers for default may be broader, reflecting sponsor accountability for performance and capital commitments.

Guarantees and Carve-Outs

Carve-outs are the primary mechanism by which lenders preserve protection in non-recourse loans. Common carve-outs cover fraud, misrepresentation, bankruptcy, and improper removal of liens. Some Florida transactions may also include specific cure periods or project milestones as carve-outs tied to asset performance.

Recourse financing typically involves guarantees—either full or limited—that attach to the sponsor or a parent entity. Even with recourse, lenders may accept limited guarantees or guaranties tied to specific milestones, asset types, or cash-flow tests, especially if the sponsor has a proven capital plan and supportive equity commitments.

Cross-Collateral and Cross-Default

Cross-collateralization can be more common in portfolios or in markets with a high degree of asset interdependence. In Florida, cross-default language can materially affect sponsor liquidity if one asset underperforms, even when others perform well. Sponsors should carefully model potential impact on liquidity and project viability under both recourse and non-recourse scenarios.

Sponsor and Lender Decision Points

  • Stabilized assets often favor non-recourse structures, while development or redevelopment may require recourse or enhanced guarantees to secure financing.
  • A sponsor with a strong balance sheet and demonstrated execution capability can influence lender comfort in recourse deals and may gain favorable pricing or flexibility.
  • Non-recourse financing tends to come with carefully calibrated leverage and cash-flow tests. Recourse may offer more flexible leverage in exchange for sponsor exposure.
  • In Florida, elevated insurance costs and reserve requirements can affect debt service and covenants. A lender’s willingness to adjust reserves or DSCR thresholds can be a deciding factor.
  • If a transaction contemplates future recapitalization, the chosen structure should align with expected liquidity events and sponsor liquidity needs.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Underestimating reserve requirements: In Florida, reserve adequacy for insurance deductibles, tax exposure, and capital expenditures is critical, especially for non-recourse deals where leverage sensitivity is high.
  • Over-optimistic cash-flow projections: If underwriting assumes aggressive rent growth or occupancy without hedges, the asset can fail to meet covenant tests, triggering defaults or unwanted recourse discussions.
  • Misunderstanding carve-outs: Sponsors should ensure they understand the scope and triggers of bad boy carve-outs and how they interact with project milestones and funding schedules.
  • Ignoring cross-asset implications: In portfolios or cross-collateral deals, a weakness in one asset can disproportionately impact others. Plan for liquidity and covenant relief if diversification proves insufficient.
  • Misalignment of equity and debt plans: Equity commitments should be aligned with debt service needs and potential recourse exposure to avoid funding gaps during execution.

Practical Conclusion

For Florida commercial properties, choosing between non-recourse and recourse financing is a nuanced decision driven by asset quality, sponsor capability, and market conditions. Non-recourse structures protect the sponsor while requiring disciplined underwriting, risk management, and robust reserves. Recourse financing can provide execution flexibility and potentially favorable terms in certain scenarios but transfers more liability to the sponsor and its principals. In Florida’s dynamic CRE landscape, a careful balance of covenants, guarantees, and risk allocation is essential to protect value across acquisitions, developments, recapitalizations, and ongoing financing needs.
