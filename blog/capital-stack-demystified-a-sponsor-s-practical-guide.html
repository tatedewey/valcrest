Capital Stack Demystified: A Sponsor’s Practical Guide

A clear, practical tour of each capital-stack layer—what it does, how it behaves in good and bad cycles, and what sponsors should negotiate to align economics and control.

By Tate Dewey, Co-Founder of Valcrest Capital · August 15, 2026

When I talk with sponsors and institutional capital providers, the same questions come up: how do the different layers of the capital stack interact, who owns which risks, and how should a sponsor think about negotiating terms beyond price? This article breaks the capital stack into four practical layers—senior debt, mezzanine debt, preferred equity, and common equity—and describes how they work together across underwriting, operations, distress and exit.

Why the capital stack matters

The capital stack is the operating architecture of a commercial real estate deal. It determines priority of payments, control rights, return expectations, downside protection, and ultimately whether a sponsor can execute its business plan. Thinking of the stack as a set of contracts—each with economics, covenants and remedies—helps sponsors optimize for liquidity, flexibility and alignment with capital partners.

Senior debt: the backbone

Senior debt sits at the top of the stack in priority. It is typically a first-lien loan secured by the property and carries the lowest headline cost of capital. From a sponsor’s perspective, senior debt’s value is predictability: scheduled principal and interest payments, defined covenants, and a clear remedy pathway in default.

Key characteristics

  • Priority: First lien on the asset and the primary cash flow claimant.
  • Cost: Generally the lowest coupon but highly dependent on leverage, property type, and lender risk appetite.
  • Covenants: Debt service coverage ratios (DSCR), loan-to-value (LTV), and reporting requirements are common.
  • Term: Fixed maturity with possible amortization, and often calls and prepayment mechanics.

Senior lenders focus on downside protection. They underwrite the loan to a stressed cash flow and value, and they expect a sponsor to maintain sufficient cushion. When negotiating senior debt, sponsors should think beyond the rate: prepayment terms, covenant thresholds, reporting cadence, reserve requirements, and lender consent triggers can materially affect operating flexibility.

Mezzanine debt: bridging equity gaps

Mezzanine debt sits below senior debt and above equity. It is generally unsecured or secured by equity interests in the borrowing entity rather than the property itself. Mezz provides incremental leverage with less dilution to equity, but it comes at a higher cost and often tighter control features than senior debt.

Key characteristics

  • Subordination: Mezz is subordinated to senior debt, meaning repayment priority favors the senior lender.
  • Economics: Higher coupon than senior debt; sometimes includes payment-in-kind (PIK) structures or equity kickers.
  • Control: Mezz lenders often require intercreditor agreements, step-in rights, or approval on major decisions.
  • Use cases: Gap financing for acquisitions, recapitalizations, or to fund value-add programs without further diluting common equity.

Mezzanine lenders care about the intercreditor relationship. From a sponsor’s view, negotiating subordination and standstill periods is critical: you want to preserve breathing room for a turnaround while giving the mezz lender adequate protection. Mezz terms that look attractive on price can become restrictive if they grant broad step-in rights or accelerate on discovery of certain defaults.

Preferred equity: a hybrid between debt and equity

Preferred equity occupies a gray area. It ranks after mezz debt in the capital stack but before common equity. Preferred equity holders receive preferential cash distributions—often a fixed or stated return—before common equity receives any proceeds. Preferred structures give sponsors access to capital with fewer covenants than mezzanine debt but more expensive than senior debt.

Key characteristics

  • Payment priority: Preferred returns and catch-up provisions are common, creating a predictable payment stream similar to debt.
  • Control and governance: Preferred holders may accept limited governance in exchange for economic priority, but they often impose protective provisions tied to budgets, distributions, or refinancing events.
  • Economics: Returns are typically fixed or accrued and may be cumulative or non-cumulative; equity upside is sometimes shared via participation rights.
  • Flexibility: Preferred can be designed to be redeemable, convertible, or perpetual—each choice affects sponsor flexibility and investor alignment.

Preferred equity is attractive when sponsors want to avoid the mechanical acceleration or step-in mechanics common to mezzanine debt while still providing investors a contractual preferred return. Negotiation points that matter: whether the preferred return is paid current or accrued, conversion mechanics, distribution waterfalls, and consent thresholds for major transactions.

Common equity: residual upside and the most risk

Common equity is the most junior layer and the holder of residual economics. This is where sponsors and passive equity partners take the upside after all prior claimants are paid. Common equity also carries the first losses—meaning it is the most exposed in a downturn but also the source of outsized returns when the business plan succeeds.

Key characteristics

  • Residual claim: Paid after senior, mezz, and preferred layers; absorbs the first losses.
  • Governance: Sponsors usually retain control or special manager rights, subject to investor protections.
  • Economics: Returns are variable and typically delivered via cash flow splits, promoted interests, and carried interest structures.
  • Alignment: Common equity aligns sponsors’ incentives with asset performance and exit execution.

Common equity negotiations center on promote percentages, distribution waterfalls, capital call mechanics, and sponsor fees. Sponsors must balance achieving attractive upside for themselves with offering sufficient returns to attract limited partners.

How the layers interact: practical mechanics

The capital stack is more than a priority chart; it is an operating ecosystem. Here are the practical ways the layers interact throughout a hold period and at transition events.

Cash flow allocations and waterfalls

Cash flow is allocated first to senior debt service, then to mezz and preferred obligations, and finally to common equity according to the waterfall. Details matter: are preferred payments cumulative? Does mezz allow PIK? What are the thresholds for releasing reserves? Sponsors should map cash flow under base case, downside, and distress scenarios to understand who gets paid and when.

Control and decision-making

Control rights are distributed to protect capital providers according to seniority. Senior lenders protect collateral and covenant compliance; mezz and preferred investors protect their economic priority with consent rights on asset sales, refinancing, and significant capital projects. Sponsors should aim for clarity: which decisions trigger lender consent, and what cure or notice periods apply?

Default and remedies

In default, remedies cascade according to priority. Senior lenders typically have foreclosure rights; mezz lenders may foreclose on equity pledges; preferred investors may have conversion or acceleration rights. Intercreditor agreements define standstill periods and who can step in. Sponsors should anticipate these pathways and negotiate reasonable cure opportunities and dispute resolution mechanics.

Refinance and exit timing

Exit events crystallize the hierarchy: sale proceeds repay senior debt first, then subordinated layers and owners. Refinancing may require subordinate layer consents or payoff provisions. Sponsors should understand how prepayment premiums, defeasance requirements, or payoff mechanics impact net proceeds at exit.

Practical negotiation points sponsors regularly face

  1. Flexibility vs cost: Lower-cost capital often brings more restrictive covenants. Decide which operational freedoms are worth paying for and where you need flexibility.
  2. Intercreditor clarity: Explicit intercreditor agreements reduce ambiguity in stress scenarios. Define standstill periods, step-in mechanics, and enforcement rights up front.
  3. Payment mechanics: Are preferred returns cumulative? Is mezz interest payable in cash or PIK? These choices affect cash flow and gearing over time.
  4. Refinance and sale consents: Set thresholds for consent or automatic release mechanics to avoid being blocked from opportunistic exits.
  5. Governance thresholds: Carve out day-to-day operational decisions from major decisions requiring investor consent to preserve agility.

Scenario planning: align terms with likely outcomes

Sponsors should model at least three outcomes—base case, downside (slower lease-up or revenue pressure), and upside (faster stabilization or cap rate compression). For each outcome, map cash flows, debt coverage, and distribution waterfalls. That exercise reveals which capital partners are most likely to rake returns or suffer losses, and where sponsor incentives may misalign with investors.

Alignment is not just about price; it's about timing, remedies, and the story you and your capital partners tell about the business plan.

Common pitfalls and how to avoid them

  • Over-levering to chase return: High leverage can boost IRR in a favorable outcome but reduces tolerance for variance. Stress-test underwriting thoroughly.
  • Vague intercreditor language: Ambiguous step-in or cure provisions create drama in distress. Insist on specificity.
  • Undervalued operational covenants: Tight reporting and reserve requirements can be manageable if negotiated sensibly; don’t gloss over these line items.
  • Ignoring refinancing mechanics: Prepayment penalties, defeasance, or required cash reserves can erode exit proceeds—model them into your sensitivity analyses.

Conclusion: structure to match probable outcomes

For sponsors, capital-stack optimization is an exercise in matching likely outcomes to the right mix of economics, control and flexibility. Senior debt provides predictability; mezz and preferred bridge gaps between debt and equity with varied control and cost; common equity captures residual upside and assumes first-loss risk. Negotiating beyond headline rates—covenants, intercreditor mechanics, payment mechanics and consent thresholds—determines whether the stack enables the business plan or impedes it under pressure.

Think of the stack as a living document: model the paths you expect and those you hope to avoid, then structure capital to preserve the path you need. Clear, precise terms reduce ambiguity when markets move.

Practical next steps: map your cash flows under multiple scenarios; prioritize the three negotiation points that matter most to your operational plan; and negotiate intercreditor clarity before committing to pricing you can’t live with in stress.
