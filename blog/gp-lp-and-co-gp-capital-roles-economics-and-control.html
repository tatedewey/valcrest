GP, LP and Co-GP Capital: Roles, Economics and Control

As a co-founder of Valcrest Capital, I lay out the practical differences in roles, economics and control among GP equity, LP equity and co-GP capital so sponsors and allocators can structure clearer deals.

By Tate Dewey, Co-Founder of Valcrest Capital · August 16, 2026

Author: Tate Dewey, Co-Founder of Valcrest Capital

Why the distinction matters

In commercial real estate transactions the words "equity" and "sponsor" are often used interchangeably, but not all equity is the same. GP equity, LP equity and co-GP capital have different positions in the stack, different economics, and different governance implications. Confusion among these roles creates misaligned incentives, execution friction and unexpected dilution at critical moments. This article clarifies practical differences and when each source is typically appropriate.

Definitions and where they sit in the capital stack

At a high level:

  • GP equity is the sponsor’s ownership stake and operational commitment. It represents the sponsor’s aligned capital and typically entitles the sponsor to promote or carried interest above a preferred return.
  • LP equity is passive capital provided by limited partners. LP equity usually sits senior to sponsor-level promote in the economic waterfall and expects limited governance rights in exchange for return protection.
  • Co-GP capital refers to outside capital that sits alongside, or inside, the sponsor layer. It can be structured in different ways—co-invest alongside GP, a junior equity tranche, or an agreement to share GP economics—and is often used to bridge sponsor funding gaps or to add operating expertise.

Think of LP equity as the pool of money buying the core economic exposure, GP equity as the sponsor’s skin in the game and incentive layer, and co-GP as a flexible tool that can augment or substitute parts of the sponsor layer.

Roles and responsibilities

Roles are governed by the operating agreement, but patterns are consistent:

  • General Partner (GP) - Responsible for sourcing, executing and managing the asset day-to-day. The GP typically has delegated decision-making authority and operational control, subject to consent items defined in the partnership agreement.
  • Limited Partner (LP) - Provides capital with limited involvement in daily operations. LPs expect reporting, defined consent rights on major actions, and protections that preserve economic priority.
  • Co-GP - May take an active operating role, a minority governance role, or be a purely capital partner at the GP layer. The specific responsibilities depend on the negotiated structure—some co-GPs operate assets, others provide balance-sheet flexibility or access to relationships.

Economic structures and incentives

Economics are where the differences most sharply affect outcomes.

LP equity economics

LP equity normally receives priority return structures designed to protect downside and provide a predictable return profile. Common elements include:

  • Preferred return (pref)—an annual return paid to LPs before promote kicks in.
  • Return of capital—LPs usually get capital back before split of residual proceeds in many waterfalls.
  • Promote / carry—the GP receives a disproportionate share of upside above the preferred hurdle as compensation for value creation.

GP equity economics

GP equity typically has a smaller pro rata ownership but a larger share of upside via promote. The sponsor’s economics are structured to reward outperformance. GP equity is also exposed to first-loss scenarios and often funds shortfalls such as capital calls.

Co-GP economics

Co-GP capital is highly flexible and can be structured to mimic GP economics, sit between GP and LP layers, or take a hybrid form. Typical outcomes include:

  • A share of the promote in exchange for sponsor-level capital.
  • Junior equity returns that carry higher preferred rates but limited influence.
  • Structures where co-GP capital is paid a fixed or preferred return and then converts or participates in upside—useful when a co-GP wants downside protection with upside participation.

Because co-GP capital can be fashioned to meet sponsor or investor needs, it is important to document its priority and distribution mechanics clearly to avoid unintended waterfall dilution.

Control and governance implications

Control and governance flow from roles and from how capital is structured. Key considerations:

  • Voting and consent items—LPs typically secure consent rights for major items (sale, refinancing, additional leverage, changes in business plan). GPs retain day-to-day authority but are constrained by the partnership governance framework.
  • Co-GP voting—co-GPs can be granted varying degrees of governance: full GP votes, limited operating votes, or purely economic rights. If a co-GP receives GP votes, that can materially change sponsor control dynamics.
  • Removal and remediation—LPs often negotiate remedies if the GP materially defaults. Co-GP arrangements should clarify cure rights, transfer restrictions and what constitutes a change of control at the sponsor level.

Negotiation of governance terms reflects risk appetite: institutions trade some return for control protections; sponsors trade some control for capital and balance-sheet relief.

Common use cases and practical trade-offs

When to use each capital type depends on sponsor needs and investor objectives:

  • Use GP equity when: the sponsor wants to demonstrate alignment of interest, retain full control of operations and capture the promote upside. GP equity is also the first line of defense for lenders and LPs assessing sponsor skin in the game.
  • Use LP equity when: the project needs passive institutional capital with scale—LPs provide core funding, expect governance protections, and accept a primarily financial role.
  • Use co-GP capital when: the sponsor needs additional sponsor-level capital, wants to reduce personal capital exposure, or wants to bring an operating partner into the deal without fully diluting LP economics. Co-GP can also be attractive for recapitalizations and holdperiod financing flexibility.

Trade-offs to watch:

  • Dilution vs execution—bringing co-GP capital can reduce the sponsor’s promote or require sharing economics, but it can enable larger transactions and reduce execution risk tied to capital constraints.
  • Control vs capital—granting co-GP governance rights can stabilize operations but diminishes unilateral control; decide which matters more for the business plan.
  • Complexity—adding a co-GP can complicate waterfalls, reporting and tax allocation. Make sure agreements are clear on distribution waterfalls and default consequences.

Structuring mechanics sponsors and investors should negotiate

Practical clauses and mechanics to focus on in documentation:

  1. Waterfall order and definitions—explicitly define pref, return of capital, promote tiers, and whether co-GP participates in catch-up mechanisms.
  2. Capital call and cure provisions—clarify sponsor obligations for shortfalls, whether co-GP can cure, and the pricing of non-participation.
  3. Governance and consent thresholds—list consent items and set vote thresholds for material actions; define co-GP voting rights plainly.
  4. Transfer limitations—protect the partnership from unwanted third-party transfers by setting approval standards.
  5. Exit mechanics and secondary sales—agree upfront how sponsor and co-GP economics are treated on sale, refinance or recapitalization; address tag/drag rights for liquidity events.

How institutional allocators typically view each source

Institutional investors look for predictability and governance. LPs expect comprehensive reporting, tight consent mechanics on major value drivers, and transparency on sponsor capital positions. Co-GP capital is evaluated on how it changes the effective economics and governance: does it meaningfully alter the sponsor’s alignment, and does it increase or decrease execution risk? Sponsors should be prepared to walk through waterfall examples and downside scenarios.

Practical negotiation tips

  • Run waterfall models for multiple outcomes—show LPs and potential co-GPs how returns split at base, stress, and upside cases.
  • Be explicit about timing—when are distributions made, and how are capital calls handled in interim periods?
  • Protect the business plan—if a co-GP brings operational value, define roles, KPIs and decision gates to avoid later disputes.
  • Keep agreements pragmatic—complexity can cost time and capital; prioritize the clauses that materially affect alignment and execution.

Clear documentation and straightforward economic examples reduce friction and preserve execution optionality.

Concise practical conclusion

GP equity signals alignment and operational control, LP equity supplies the bulk of passive funding and governance protections, and co-GP capital is a flexible sponsor-layer tool that can bridge capital needs or bring operating expertise. The right mix depends on execution priorities: preserve control when operational decisions must be fast, add co-GP capital when sponsor funding capacity is constrained, and rely on LP equity when scale and predictability matter. In every case, negotiate explicit waterfall mechanics, voting rights, and cure/transfer provisions so economics and control are clear from day one.

If you are negotiating any of these structures, focus early efforts on waterfall clarity, consent thresholds and the practical mechanics of capital calls and distributions—those items drive outcomes more than labels.
