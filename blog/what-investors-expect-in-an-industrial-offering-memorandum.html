What Investors Expect in an Industrial Offering Memorandum

A practical framework detailing the sections and disclosures investors look for in an industrial OM, bridging sponsors and capital providers.

By Tate Dewey, Co-Founder of Valcrest Capital · October 2, 2026

Direct opening explanation: In industrial acquisitions, the offering memorandum (OM) is the primary document institutional and private capital teams rely on to evaluate an opportunity. The OM must translate a sponsor thesis into measurable, defendable numbers and clearly describe risk, operations, and market context. From the CRE capital markets perspective and aligned with Valcrest Capital approach, the OM should demonstrate how the asset will perform under different scenarios, what financing is feasible, and how exit or recapitalization paths might unfold.

From the investor perspective, the OM should begin with an executive summary that articulates the investment thesis, expected returns, and the capital plan, followed by asset level disclosures and market data that support those projections. Clarity, consistency, and a credible risk framework are non negotiable in CRE capital markets.

Asset level disclosures and financials

  • Property overview: location, total rentable area, building type, and submarket significance to logistics and supply chain flexibility.
  • Lease and tenancy: current rent roll, occupancy, lease terms, rollover risk, and concentration by tenant or sector.
  • Operating statements: current NOI, operating expenses, real estate taxes, insurance, utilities, and management fees summarized with supporting schedules.
  • Capital expenditures and reserves: planned capex, deferred maintenance, reserve levels, and a cash flow funded improvement plan.
  • Debt and financing assumptions: proposed leverage, loan terms, interest rate environment, amortization, maturity schedule, and covenants.
  • Valuation and returns: base case returns, cap rate assumptions, discount rates, and equity multiples with clearly labeled scenarios.
  • Tax and accounting considerations: depreciation schedules, 1031 implications, and partner tax allocations presented without legal advice.
  • Asset condition and environmental risk: Phase I assessments, known issues, remediation plans, and ongoing environmental management.
  • Title, liens, and permits: status of title, encumbrances, zoning compliance, and any operating permits tied to the property usage.

Market context and asset level drivers

  • Market fundamentals: submarket supply and demand dynamics, occupancy trends, utilization metrics, and absorption expectations.
  • Logistics and demand drivers: e commerce fulfillment, last mile, cold storage, and regional distribution patterns shaping rent and yield profiles.
  • Location attributes: access to highways, intermodal facilities, air and rail connectivity, labor availability, and competitive positioning.
  • Rent growth and volatility: stated assumptions for near term and long term rent trajectories, including macroeconomic sensitivity considerations.

Financing structure and capital strategy

  • Debt terms and structure: target loan-to-value, debt yield, DSCR thresholds, interest rate type, amortization, and potential coverage protections.
  • Equity framework: sponsorship economics, waterfall concepts, preferred equity considerations, and alignment with institutional equity expectations without implying guarantees.
  • Hold period and exit plan: projected hold duration, potential refinance events, and exit scenarios across varying market conditions.
  • Recapitalization options: potential paths for recapitalization including mezzanine or equity recapitalizations if applicable to the asset type and strategy.

Due diligence and risk management disclosures

  • Representations and warranties: scope and depth of sponsor and asset representations, including any known material facts that could affect value realization.
  • Compliance and tax posture: regulatory considerations, tax structuring implications, and reporting standards observed in the OM.
  • Environmental, social, and governance notes: material ESG considerations where relevant to logistics and industrial tenants.
  • Operational risk disclosures: management contracts, vendor dependencies, and business continuity plans for key operating elements.

Common mistakes to avoid

  • Overly optimistic rent growth or unrealistic capex assumptions without credible support or sensitivity analysis.
  • Incomplete data rooms or inconsistent schedules that hinder diligence speed and credibility.
  • Ambiguity around debt terms, covenants, or reserve requirements that create ambiguity for lenders and equity.
  • Underreporting tenant concentration or mischaracterizing lease maturity profiles that masks risk concentration.
  • Failing to distinguish between base case projections and scenario analyses, making it hard to compare outcomes.

Decision points for investors and lenders

  • Do the asset level financials align with the stated investment thesis and cap rate expectations?
  • Are the financing assumptions consistent with lender appetite in the current CRE capital markets, including LTV, DSCR, and leverage transitions?
  • Is the market analysis robust and reflective of current and forward looking supply demand dynamics?
  • Are risk disclosures comprehensive and transparent, including environmental, title, and permitting considerations?
  • Is there a clear path to exit or recapitalization, and how do market shifts affect that path?

Practical conclusions and next steps

A robust industrial offering memorandum balances asset level financial clarity with market grounded rationale. It should present credible leverage and return expectations, explicit risk disclosures, and a clear financing framework that lenders and equity sources can model. For sponsors pursuing industrial acquisitions, developments, or recapitalizations, adopting the structure outlined here can accelerate discussions with institutional partners and improve the efficiency of capital markets interactions.
