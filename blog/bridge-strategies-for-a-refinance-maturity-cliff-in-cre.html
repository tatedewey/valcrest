Bridge Strategies for a Refinance Maturity Cliff in CRE

When a CRE loan nears maturity with refinancing unclear, tactical bridge financing and disciplined lender negotiations can bridge the gap without derailing value.

By Tate Dewey, Co-Founder of Valcrest Capital · September 29, 2026

When a commercial real estate loan approaches its maturity and a clear refinancing path hasn’t materialized, sponsors face a liquidity and value risk that can rapidly unfold. Tate Dewey, Co-Founder of Valcrest Capital, brings a pragmatic framework from the CRE capital markets to help sponsors and lenders close the gap with purpose-built bridge strategies. This article reflects Valcrest Capital’s approach to financing, acquisitions, developments, and recapitalizations, guided by a disciplined, market-informed process. Samuel Aureliano, Managing Partner at Valcrest Capital, is part of the team that develops these strategies, without implying guarantees or specific transactions. The goal is to illuminate options, trade-offs, and decision points that a sponsor can use to navigate a looming maturity cliff.

What is a refinance maturity cliff and why action is required

A refinance maturity cliff occurs when a loan’s stated maturity date arrives before a sponsor can secure new financing on terms that preserve value. In CRE capital markets, the consequences can include accelerated collateral, forced asset dispositions at unfavorable pricing, or disrupted occupancy and operations. Acting promptly to bridge the gap preserves optionality, protects capital stacks, and keeps value creation plans on track. Key questions to answer early include:

  • What is the exact maturity date and any extension rights or penalties?
  • What is the current DSCR, occupancy, rent-roll stability, and NOI sensitivity to scenario changes?
  • What are the options for extending, substituting, or recapitalizing debt and how will they affect leverage and control?
  • What interim liquidity is required to sustain operations and capex goals during the bridge period?

Bridge financing options: short-term paths to bridge the gap

Short-term bank or specialty-lender bridge loans

These facilities typically provide fast funding with relatively straightforward covenants, designed to cover a 6–18 month gap. Key considerations include:

  • Interest rates and points versus extension options and prepayment terms
  • Eligible collateral and whether the loan sits on a blanket lien or is limited to a specific asset
  • Extension flexibility, often tied to achieving certain operating metrics or delivering a plan

Mezzanine debt and preferred equity

Mezzanine and preferred equity can fill a senior debt gap while preserving sponsor control. They carry higher cost of capital but can be deployed quickly to support a refinancing or recapitalization plan. Considerations include:

  • Structural seniority and potential subordination risk
  • Interest or distribution preferences and catch-up mechanics
  • Impact on reserve requirements and debt service coverage after funding

Asset-based lending and holdco financing

Asset-based lenders focus on collateral value and cash flow supporting the loan. This may be attractive when cash flow is volatile or when other sources are constrained. Look for:

  • Credit against stabilized rent roll, accounts receivable, and other assets
  • Ratios tied to collateral coverage rather than solely to DSCR
  • Fees and readiness to close quickly with limited pre-closing conditions

Sale-leaseback and structured recaps

For properties with strong occupancy and long-term tenants, a sale-leaseback can release capital while preserving operational control. Important factors include:

  • Lease terms, termination risk, and rent escalations
  • Tax and legal structure implications for the seller
  • Post-transaction debt capacity and equity requirements

Incremental equity and recapitalization strategies

When debt options are constrained, an equity backstop or joint-venture recapitalization can restore balance sheet flexibility. Consider:

  • Cost of equity versus debt in the capital stack
  • Control rights, veto provisions, and governance implications
  • Alignment of sponsor incentives with new capital partners

Development or interim construction financing

If value can be unlocked through near-term enhancements or repositioning, interim construction or development financing can bridge to a permanent solution. Focus areas include:

  • Milestone-based funding tied to lease-up or capex completion
  • Clear draw schedules and cash management covenants
  • Defined path to a longer-term financing plan once improvements deliver measurable value

Lender negotiation playbook: how to structure the ask

  • Present a well-supported refinancing narrative: current performance, stress testing, and a credible plan to restore or grow DSCR.
  • Include a data room with rent rolls, lease amendments, tenant credits, operating statements, capex plans, and pro forma scenarios that show sensitivity to occupancy and rent changes.
  • Offer extension or replacement options. Lenders value clarity on what comes next and under what terms.
  • Define covenants pragmatically. In exchange for liquidity, lenders may accept scaled reporting, more permissive debt-coverage tests, or step-down triggers.
  • Negotiate fees and prepayment economics. Balance upfront points, non-cash charges, and potential extension costs with the certainty of funding.
  • Clarify recourse and liability. If non-recourse is not feasible, structure limited-recourse or carve-outs with clear remedies.
  • Align with sponsor and lender timelines. A well-timed, decision-driven process reduces the risk of a cliff edge closing.

Operational discipline during the bridge period

  • Stabilize cash flow: optimize occupancy, renewals, and rent collections; control tenant delinquencies with proactive communication.
  • Preserve capital stack integrity: prioritize essential capex that directly protects NOI and tenant retention.
  • Maintain lender-friendly reporting: establish a predictable cadence of financials, leasing metrics, and reserves.
  • Prepare for a clean close: align the bridge plan with the eventual permanent financing strategy and the expected capital stack.

Common mistakes to avoid

  • Underestimating extension fees or over-optimistic redemption timelines, which can erode value and strip optionality.
  • Entering a bridge without a credible permanent-financing plan, creating a longer-than-necessary bridge and higher carrying costs.
  • Failing to assemble a comprehensive data room and mispricing the risk in a stressed scenario.
  • Assuming all lenders will extend on identical terms; terms are highly contract-specific and time-bound.
  • Neglecting intervention points with tenants, operations, and asset management that impact near-term cash flow.

Conclusion: a practical path through the cliff

Short-term bridge strategies to solve a refinance maturity cliff require disciplined planning, credible data, and a flexible approach to capital structure. By evaluating the full spectrum of options—from bridge loans and mezzanine structures to asset-based facilities and recapitalizations—sponsors can preserve optionality, protect asset value, and position the asset for a durable, long-term financing solution.
