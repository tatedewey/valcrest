Capital Stack Options for Financing Last-Mile Industrial Properties

This guide explains practical capital stack configurations for last-mile industrial assets, aligning sponsor goals with institutional capital expectations to move deals forward.

By Tate Dewey, Co-Founder of Valcrest Capital · September 14, 2026

Written by Tate Dewey, Co-Founder of Valcrest Capital, with a framework that reflects how CRE sponsors and institutional capital providers evaluate transactions in the modern market. Samuel Aureliano, Managing Partner at Valcrest Capital, supports the perspective from the firm’s overarching CRE capital markets view.

Direct opening: why the capital stack matters for last-mile industrial

Last-mile industrial properties sit at the sharp end of the logistics cycle, bridging e-commerce demand with delivery speed. Financing such assets demands a thoughtful capital stack that balances leverage, cost of capital, and speed to lease-up or disposition. The goal is not to maximize debt at any cost, but to preserve optionality—so sponsors can adapt to leasing performance, rate shifts, and potential recapitalizations without compromising returns.

Key Components of the Last-Mile Cap Stack

While every deal has its unique elements, the core components of a cap stack tend to converge around four primary categories: equity, preferred/equity-like securities, senior debt, and subordinated debt or structured debt. Each serves a different purpose in risk allocation and return profile.

  • Sponsor equity or sponsor-equity tranche provides the “skin in the game.” It aligns interests, supports execution risk, and often facilitates arranging more favorable terms from lenders.
  • Preferred equity (or mezzanine-like structures) offers a fixed return priority over common equity while not fully diluting control. This layer can bridge target leverage gaps without increasing senior debt costs excessively.
  • Senior debt forms the base financing line and typically comes from life companies, banks, or national lenders. It sets the primary loan-to-value target and carries the most protective covenants.
  • Structured debt or subordinated debt provides additional leverage while maintaining a disciplined risk profile. This can include mezzanine loans, PIK (payment-in-kind) features, or construction bridge facilities tied to pre-leasing milestones.

Common Configurations for Last-Mile Industrial

Below are representative configurations you’re likely to encounter. Each is designed to optimize speed to market, keep capital costs aligned with risk, and position the asset for stable or recapitalized ownership later.

Configuration A: Stabilized Asset with Traditional Debt

This is the simplest, most straightforward approach for a fully leased or stabilized asset. A robust senior-debt tranche funds the bulk of the cost, sponsor equity covers the remainder, and there’s little or no subordinated debt. The emphasis is on solid rent coverage, durable tenant quality, and a reserve framework that buffers rate and lease-risk fluctuations.

  • Senior debt from a bank or life insurer with a conventional LTV target (often 60–70% at close, depending on quality and location).
  • Sponsor equity sufficient to meet reserve requirements and deliver a compelling cash-on-cash return.
  • Limited or no reliance on mezzanine or preferred equity if the pro forma shows strong occupancy and predictable cash flow.

Configuration B: Levered Acquisition with Mezzanine or Preferred Equity

When leverage needs to be higher to achieve target returns or to preserve liquidity for capex, mezzanine or preferred equity becomes part of the stack. This configuration is common in markets with tight supply and strong demand drivers, where lenders may require additional structural cushions to accommodate leasing risk.

  • Senior debt at a conservative LTV, paired with a preferred-equity layer or mezzanine debt to reach higher total leverage.
  • Fixed or step-up preferred return in the subordinate tier, with distributions prioritized before common equity.
  • Sponsor equity still present to maintain alignment and to absorb any early-overrun costs or capex needs.

Configuration C: Construction or Bridge Financing

For developments that need to season during construction or pre-leasing, a bridge facility paired with a forward-perm or construction-to-perm loan is common. The bridge funds construction and early lease activity, while a long-term lender commits to a perm loan once stabilization metrics are met.

  • Construction loan with milestones tied to leasing/perm readiness.
  • Forward or perm loan commitment contingent on pre-leasing thresholds and delivery timing.
  • Inline reserves and rate-hedging strategies to manage interest-rate exposure during construction.

Configuration D: Recapitalization and Equity Recapital

As asset performance unfolds, sponsors may pursue a recapitalization to improve liquidity or unlock upside. A sanctioned equity recap may involve re-pricing, adding a preferred layer, or bringing in new equity partners while maintaining operational control or governance rights in alignment with pre-agreed terms.

  • Add a preferred-equity layer to reset the return profile for new or existing investors.
  • Employer of a structured debt component to optimize cost of capital without over-leveraging.
  • Clear, pre-defined triggers for capital events and distributions to protect both sponsor and investors.

Creative Options and Contingencies

Beyond traditional configurations, sponsors and lenders increasingly explore options that preserve optionality and speed. These strategies are particularly relevant for last-mile assets where lease-up trajectories can be highly variable and market cycles compress or expand quickly.

  • Forward-funding and sale-leaseback arrangements can accelerate capital deployment and provide certainty for development timelines, while preserving cash flow through lease-backed structures.
  • Sale-leaseback with credit tenant enhancements can attract capital by delivering long, stable cash flows and reducing redevelopment risk.
  • Construction-to-perm with rate hedges protects against rate volatility during build and stabilizes debt service post-stabilization.

Decision Points: Sponsors and Lenders

When evaluating a cap stack, both sides focus on a common set of decision drivers. Understanding these can help structure discussions that move deals forward more efficiently.

  1. Lease-up and occupancy risk: What is the expected timeline to stabilization, and how sensitive is the pro forma to slower leasing activity?
  2. Location and market fundamentals: How does proximity to population centers and distribution corridors affect exit options and rent growth?
  3. Capital cost vs. return: What is the target return for each stakeholder, and how does the stack balance risk-adjusted yield?
  4. Control and governance: What rights do lenders or preferred investors hold if performance drifts, and how will distributions be prioritized?
  5. Recapitalization potential: Are there clear, agreed triggers and pathways for future capitalization events?

Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Over-leveraging to chase IRR targets without adequate cash reserves or hedging, risking cash shortfalls during ramp-up.
  • Underestimating lease-up risk in last-mile corridors, leading to delayed stabilization and stressed debt-service coverage.
  • Inadequate reserve planning for tenant improvements, vacancies, or rate resets, which can derail distributions to equity holders.
  • Failing to align lender covenants with sponsor milestones or to clearly map out recapitalization paths before closing.

Practical Checklist: Review a Cap Stack Before Closing

  1. Clarify all debt tranches, including interest rate type (fixed vs. floating), tenor, amortization, and maturity schedule.
  2. Define pre-stabilization milestones for construction or lease-up and attach them to debt draw schedules.
  3. Set clear cash reserves for debt service, capital expenditures, and tenant improvements.
  4. Document distribution waterfalls, priority of payments, and protections for equity and lenders.
  5. Articulate exit strategies and recapitalization options with realistic triggers and timelines.

Conclusion: Aligning Risk, Speed, and Return

The most effective capital stacks for last-mile industrial assets balance strong sponsor alignment with disciplined lender risk management. A well-constructed mix of sponsor equity, preferred equity, and thoughtfully structured debt can shorten time to lease-up, protect against rate shocks, and preserve optionality for future recaps or exits. For sponsors and lenders alike, the goal is a clear framework that supports execution while keeping the asset’s long-term performance front and center.
